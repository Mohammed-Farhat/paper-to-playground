1. The idea

Shannon asks: given probabilities p1,…,pn for a set of possible events, how much choice is involved in seeing which one occurs? He requires three things of any such measure H: it should be continuous in the pi; it should increase with n when all outcomes are equally likely; and breaking a choice into two successive choices should give the same total as one step. Theorem 2 says only one family of functions does this: H=−K∑ipilogpi.

With log base 2 the constant K just fixes the unit, and that unit is the bit. Each term −pilog2pi is that outcome's contribution: an outcome with tiny probability contributes little (it is rarely chosen), and an outcome with probability 1 contributes nothing (there is no choice at all).

Two properties make the formula easy to sanity-check. First, H=0 exactly when one pi equals 1 and the rest are 0 — certainty means no uncertainty. Second, for fixed n, H is largest when all pi=1/n, and then H=log2n: two equally likely outcomes give 1 bit, four give 2 bits.

In the playground you set the number of outcomes and their weights. The weights are normalized into probabilities, so you can type any non-negative numbers and the page shows the resulting pi, each contribution −pilog2pi, and the total H.

Why it matters

Entropy is the currency of information theory: it is the average number of bits needed to describe an outcome, and it is the limit that compression and coding schemes approach. It also gives engineers a single number for how spread out or peaked a distribution is, which is why it reappears in coding, machine learning and statistical physics.

    4. Guided explorations

    Exploration 1: Certainty gives zero bits

    Change
    Set the preset to certain (or drag w1 to 1 and the rest to 0).
    Observe
    The left chart shows one bar of height 1 and three of height 0. Every contribution bar in the right chart has height 0, and the total H reads 0 bits.
    Why
    When one pi=1 the outcome is known in advance, so there is no choice to measure. Formally −1⋅log21=0 and the zero-probability terms are defined as 0, so the whole sum is 0 — Shannon's Property (1).

    Exploration 2: Uniform distributions give log2n bits

    Change
    Start at n=4 with the uniform preset, then change n to 2, then to 3, then to 6 (re-choosing the uniform preset each time).
    Observe
    The total H and the dashed reference line both move: n=2 gives 1 bit, n=4 gives 2 bits, n=6 gives about 2.585 bits. The 'uniform reference log2n' readout always equals the total H when the bars are level.
    Why
    For equal probabilities each term is −(1/n)log2(1/n)=(log2n)/n, and summing n of them gives log2n. This is Shannon's Property (2): more equally likely events means more uncertainty, and the growth is logarithmic, so doubling n adds exactly one bit.

    5. Misconception: Entropy is not the number of outcomes

    A distribution over 6 outcomes can have less entropy than one over 2 outcomes: if the 6-outcome distribution is very peaked, H can be near 0, while two equally likely outcomes give exactly 1 bit. Entropy measures how spread out the probability mass is, not how many outcomes exist. The maximum log2n is only reached in the uniform case.

    6. Choice, Uncertainty and Entropy. Suppose we have a set of possible events whose probabilities of occurrence are p_1, p_2, ..., p_n. These probabilities are known but that is all we know concerning which event will occur. Can we find a measure of how much "choice" is involved in the selection of the event, or of how uncertain we are of the outcome? If there is such a measure, say H(p_1, p_2, ..., p_n), it is reasonable to require of it the following properties: (1) H should be continuous in the p_i. (2) If all the p_i are equal, p_i = 1/n, then H should be a monotonic increasing function of n: with equally likely events there is more choice, or uncertainty, when there are more possible events. (3) If a choice be broken down into two successive choices, the original H should be the weighted sum of the individual values of H. Theorem 2: The only H satisfying the three above assumptions is of the form H = -K sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i log p_i, where K is a positive constant (it merely amounts to a choice of a unit of measure). Quantities of the form H = -sum p_i log p_i play a central role in information theory as measures of information, choice and uncertainty, and will be recognized as the entropy of statistical mechanics. With logarithms to the base 2, the unit is the bit. Properties of H: (1) H = 0 if and only if all the p_i but one are zero, this one having the value unity; thus only when we are certain of the outcome does H vanish, otherwise H is positive. (2) For a given n, H is a maximum and equal to log n when all the p_i are equal (i.e., 1/n); this is also intuitively the most uncertain situation. For two possibilities with probabilities p and q = 1 - p, H = -(p log p + q log q), which is plotted as a function of p in Fig. 7.
