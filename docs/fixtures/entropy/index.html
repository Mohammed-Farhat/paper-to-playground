1. The idea

Suppose you are about to observe one of n events, and you know only the probabilities p1,…,pn. Shannon asked for a number H(p1,…,pn) measuring how much choice is involved. He required three things: H is continuous in the pi; when all outcomes are equally likely H increases with n; and splitting a choice into two successive choices makes H the weighted sum of the parts.

Theorem 2 says the only function satisfying these is H=−K∑ipilogpi, with K>0 just fixing the unit. Taking base-2 logarithms makes the unit the bit.

Each term −pilog2pi is that outcome's own contribution. A very likely outcome contributes little (it is not surprising), and a very unlikely one also contributes little (it rarely happens) — the largest single contribution comes from an outcome with probability 1/e≈0.368. The total H is the sum of these contributions.

Two special cases anchor the intuition. If one outcome has probability 1, every term is zero, so H=0: certainty means no choice. If all n outcomes are equally likely, pi=1/n and H=−∑(1/n)log2(1/n)=log2n, the largest value possible for that n.

Why it matters

Entropy is the currency of information theory: it sets the minimum average number of bits needed to describe the outcome of a random source, and it is the quantity that channel capacity and coding theorems are measured against. Getting comfortable with H=−∑pilog2pi on a tiny distribution makes those later results readable.

    4. Guided explorations

    Exploration 1: Certainty gives zero bits

    Change
    Look at the distribution with one weight equal to 1 and the rest 0, then drag the other weights up a little and back to 0.
    Observe
    The H value label reads 0.000 bits and every contribution bar has height 0; as soon as any other weight becomes positive, H becomes positive.
    Why
    Every term is −pilog2pi with pi either 0 or 1, and both give 0 (using 0log0=0). Property (1) of the excerpt: only when we are certain of the outcome does H vanish.

    Exploration 2: Four equally likely outcomes give two bits

    Change
    Set n to 4 and make all four weights equal. Then change n to 2, 3, 5, 6 and watch the H label.
    Observe
    With n=4 equal weights the H label reads 2.000 bits and the solid line sits exactly on the dashed log2n line. Changing n moves the dashed line to log2n and H follows it whenever the weights stay equal.
    Why
    For equal probabilities pi=1/n, so H=−∑(1/n)log2(1/n)=log2n. With n=4 that is log24=2 bits; this is the maximum for that n, Property (2).

    5. Misconception: Entropy is not the number of outcomes

    A distribution over 6 outcomes can have less entropy than one over 2 outcomes: set n=6 with weights [1,0,0,0,0,0] and H=0, while n=2 with weights [1,1] gives H=1 bit. Entropy depends on the whole distribution, not on n alone; n only sets the ceiling log2n.

    Source excerpt given to the generator
    6. Choice, Uncertainty and Entropy. Suppose we have a set of possible events whose probabilities of occurrence are p_1, p_2, ..., p_n. These probabilities are known but that is all we know concerning which event will occur. Can we find a measure of how much "choice" is involved in the selection of the event, or of how uncertain we are of the outcome? If there is such a measure, say H(p_1, p_2, ..., p_n), it is reasonable to require of it the following properties: (1) H should be continuous in the p_i. (2) If all the p_i are equal, p_i = 1/n, then H should be a monotonic increasing function of n: with equally likely events there is more choice, or uncertainty, when there are more possible events. (3) If a choice be broken down into two successive choices, the original H should be the weighted sum of the individual values of H. Theorem 2: The only H satisfying the three above assumptions is of the form H = -K sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i log p_i, where K is a positive constant (it merely amounts to a choice of a unit of measure). Quantities of the form H = -sum p_i log p_i play a central role in information theory as measures of information, choice and uncertainty, and will be recognized as the entropy of statistical mechanics. With logarithms to the base 2, the unit is the bit. Properties of H: (1) H = 0 if and only if all the p_i but one are zero, this one having the value unity; thus only when we are certain of the outcome does H vanish, otherwise H is positive. (2) For a given n, H is a maximum and equal to log n when all the p_i are equal (i.e., 1/n); this is also intuitively the most uncertain situation. For two possibilities with probabilities p and q = 1 - p, H = -(p log p + q log q), which is plotted as a function of p in Fig. 7.
