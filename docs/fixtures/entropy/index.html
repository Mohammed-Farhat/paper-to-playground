The idea

Suppose a random experiment has n possible outcomes with probabilities p1,…,pn that sum to 1. Shannon wanted a number H(p1,…,pn) that measures how uncertain we are about the outcome. He required three things: H is continuous in the pi; if all outcomes are equally likely then H increases with n; and splitting a choice into two stages makes H the weighted sum of the parts.

Theorem 2 says the only function satisfying these is H=−K∑ipilogpi. Choosing base-2 logarithms and K=1 makes the unit the bit. Each term −pilog2pi is that outcome's contribution: an outcome with tiny probability contributes little because it rarely happens, and an outcome with probability near 1 also contributes little because it is nearly certain.

Two properties follow directly. If one outcome has probability 1 and the rest are 0, every term is 0, so H=0: certainty means no uncertainty. If all n outcomes are equally likely, pi=1/n, then H=log2n, the largest possible value for that n.

Zero probabilities need care: log20 is undefined, but plog2p→0 as p→0+, so the convention is that a zero-probability outcome contributes exactly 0 to H.

Why it matters

Entropy is the foundation of information theory, data compression, and channel capacity. Seeing it as a sum of per-outcome contributions makes clear why uniform distributions carry the most uncertainty and why certainty carries none.

    Two ways to build your intuition

    Exploration 1: Certainty versus equally likely

    Change
    Turn on 'Force equally likely', then turn it off and drag p1 back toward 1.
    Observe
    With p=(1,0,0,0) the total entropy reads 0 bits and all contribution bars vanish. With the toggle on, p=(0.25,0.25,0.25,0.25) and the total reads 2 bits, matching log24.
    Why
    A certain outcome has plog2p=0 for every term, so H=0. Equal probabilities give each term 0.25×2=0.5, and four of them sum to 2.

    Exploration 2: More outcomes, more uncertainty

    Change
    Increase n from 2 to 6 with the uniform toggle on.
    Observe
    The total entropy rises from 1 bit at n=2 to about 2.585 bits at n=6, tracking log2n; the ceiling line moves up too.
    Why
    Property (2): with equally likely events, H=log2n is monotonic increasing in n, so more possible events means more choice.
    05

    Misconception: Zero probability is not an error

    A zero-probability outcome makes log20 undefined, but the limit plog2p→0 as p→0+ means its contribution is exactly 0. The page applies this convention, so setting a slider to 0 never produces NaN or Infinity.

    Back to the paper

    Paper: A Mathematical Theory of Communication (C. E. Shannon, 1948) — https://people.math.harvard.edu/~ctm/home/text/others/shannon/entropy/entropy.pdf

    Relevant part: Section 6, Choice, Uncertainty and Entropy; Theorem 2 and the properties of H

    Supported by the source excerpt

    • Shannon requires H to be continuous in the p_i, monotonic increasing in n when all p_i are equal, and additive when a choice is broken into successive choices.
    • Theorem 2: the only H satisfying these is H = -K sum p_i log p_i, with K a positive constant fixing the unit.
    • With logarithms to base 2 the unit is the bit.
    • Property (1): H = 0 if and only if all p_i but one are zero, that one having value unity.
    • Property (2): for a given n, H is maximum and equal to log n when all p_i are equal to 1/n.
    • For two possibilities with probabilities p and q = 1-p, H = -(p log p + q log q).

    Our examples and simplifications (not from the paper)

    • The interactive sliders, the bar/contribution visual, and the two-outcome curve are our teaching aids, not figures from the paper.
    • The convention that a zero-probability term contributes 0 is the standard limit of p log p as p -> 0.

    Source excerpt given to the generator
    6. Choice, Uncertainty and Entropy. Suppose we have a set of possible events whose probabilities of occurrence are p_1, p_2, ..., p_n. These probabilities are known but that is all we know concerning which event will occur. Can we find a measure of how much "choice" is involved in the selection of the event, or of how uncertain we are of the outcome? If there is such a measure, say H(p_1, p_2, ..., p_n), it is reasonable to require of it the following properties: (1) H should be continuous in the p_i. (2) If all the p_i are equal, p_i = 1/n, then H should be a monotonic increasing function of n: with equally likely events there is more choice, or uncertainty, when there are more possible events. (3) If a choice be broken down into two successive choices, the original H should be the weighted sum of the individual values of H. Theorem 2: The only H satisfying the three above assumptions is of the form H = -K sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i log p_i, where K is a positive constant (it merely amounts to a choice of a unit of measure). Quantities of the form H = -sum p_i log p_i play a central role in information theory as measures of information, choice and uncertainty, and will be recognized as the entropy of statistical mechanics. With logarithms to the base 2, the unit is the bit. Properties of H: (1) H = 0 if and only if all the p_i but one are zero, this one having the value unity; thus only when we are certain of the outcome does H vanish, otherwise H is positive. (2) For a given n, H is a maximum and equal to log n when all the p_i are equal (i.e., 1/n); this is also intuitively the most uncertain situation. For two possibilities with probabilities p and q = 1 - p, H = -(p log p + q log q), which is plotted as a function of p in Fig. 7.
