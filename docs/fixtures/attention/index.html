1. The idea

Attention answers a simple question: for each query, which keys look similar, and how much should I copy from their values? Similarity is measured by a dot product q⋅k. A big dot product means the query and key point in similar directions.

Raw dot products can be large, and a softmax over large numbers becomes nearly one-hot with tiny gradients. Equation 1 therefore divides every score by dk before the softmax. This keeps the scores in a range where the softmax is well behaved.

The softmax turns each row of scores into a probability distribution over the keys. Those weights are then used to take a weighted average of the value rows. So the output for a query is a blend of the values, with the blend determined by similarity.

Everything here is a toy: small matrices you type in by hand. No training, no learned parameters — just the forward computation of Equation 1.

Why it matters

Scaled dot-product attention is the core operation inside every Transformer block. Understanding the shape flow and the role of the 1/dk factor is the foundation for reading the rest of the architecture.

Attention(Q,K,V)=softmax(QK⊤dk)V

    4. Guided explorations

    Exploration 1: Equal scores give uniform weights

    Change
    Look at the weights heatmap and the bar chart without changing anything first, then try changing one entry of K.
    Observe
    With all keys identical, every score is the same, so each weight is 1/3 and the row sum is 1. The output is the plain average of the three value rows. Change one key entry and the weights shift toward the matching key.
    Why
    Softmax of equal scores is uniform: ec/∑jec=1/nk. The output is therefore the mean of the value rows.

    Exploration 2: A dominant score and the role of scaling

    Change
    Turn the scaling toggle off and on, and watch the weights heatmap and the bar chart.
    Observe
    With scaling on, the first weight is large but not exactly 1. With scaling off, the raw score 16 dominates and the first weight becomes essentially 1, so the output collapses onto the first value row.
    Why
    Dividing by dk shrinks large dot products. Without it, the softmax saturates toward a one-hot distribution, which is exactly the small-gradient regime the paper warns about for large dk.

    5. Misconception: Softmax is over keys, not over queries

    The softmax in Equation 1 is applied row-wise to QK⊤, so each query gets its own distribution over the keys. Rows of A sum to one; columns generally do not. Also note that scaling changes the sharpness of the weights but never changes their shape (number of rows and columns).

    3.2.1 Scaled Dot-Product Attention. The attention used is called "Scaled Dot-Product Attention". The input consists of queries and keys of dimension d_k, and values of dimension d_v. The dot products of the query with all keys are computed, each is divided by sqrt(d_k), and a softmax function is applied to obtain the weights on the values. In practice the attention function is computed on a set of queries simultaneously, packed together into a matrix Q; the keys and values are packed into matrices K and V. The matrix of outputs is computed as: Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax(Q K^T / sqrt(d_k)) V (Equation 1). The two most commonly used attention functions are additive attention and dot-product (multiplicative) attention. Dot-product attention is identical to this algorithm except for the scaling factor 1/sqrt(d_k). Additive attention computes the compatibility function using a feed-forward network with a single hidden layer. The two are similar in theoretical complexity, but dot-product attention is faster and more space-efficient in practice because it can use optimized matrix multiplication. For small d_k the two mechanisms perform similarly, but additive attention outperforms dot-product attention without scaling for larger d_k. The authors suspect that for large d_k the dot products grow large in magnitude, pushing the softmax into regions where it has extremely small gradients; to counteract this, the dot products are scaled by 1/sqrt(d_k). Footnote 4: to illustrate why the dot products get large, assume the components of q and k are independent random variables with mean 0 and variance 1; then their dot product q . k = sum_{i=1}^{d_k} q_i k_i has mean 0 and variance d_k.
