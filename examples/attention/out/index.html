The idea

Attention answers a simple question: for each query, which values should I look at, and how much? Each query q is compared with every key kj by a dot product q⋅kj. A large dot product means the query and that key point in similar directions, so that value should matter more.

The raw dot products are scores. To turn scores into weights we apply a softmax across the keys, so the weights for one query are positive and sum to one. The output for that query is then the weighted average of the value vectors.

When the key dimension dk is large, dot products tend to grow in magnitude, which pushes the softmax into a very flat or very peaked region where gradients are tiny. The fix in Equation 1 is to divide every score by dk before the softmax. That keeps the scores at a reasonable scale so the softmax stays sensitive.

Everything here is a toy: you edit the numbers, and the page recomputes scores, weights and output exactly as Equation 1 says. No training happens.

Why it matters

Scaled dot-product attention is the core operation inside every Transformer block. Understanding how scores become weights and how weights mix values is the key to reading the rest of the architecture.

Attention(Q,K,V)=softmax(QK⊤dk)V

Equation 1: the whole operation. Scores QK⊤ are scaled by 1/dk, softmaxed row-wise into weights, then multiplied by V.

    Two ways to build your intuition

    Exploration 1: Equal scores give uniform weights

    Change
    Look at the middle heatmap and the bar chart for q1.
    Observe
    Every score in row q1 is equal, so every weight is 1/3 and the output row is the plain average of the three value rows.
    Why
    Softmax of equal scores is uniform: exp(c)/∑lexp(c)=1/m for m keys. The output is then the mean of the values.

    Exploration 2: A dominant score concentrates weight

    Change
    Now turn the scaling toggle off and watch the q1 row of the middle heatmap and the bar chart.
    Observe
    With scaling on, the weight on k1 is large but not 1. With scaling off, the largest score is multiplied by dk, the softmax becomes sharper, and the weight on k1 moves closer to 1, so the output row moves closer to v1.
    Why
    Dividing by dk shrinks the score spread. Without it, large scores dominate the softmax and the weights saturate, which is exactly the small-gradient problem the scaling is meant to fix.
    05

    Misconception: Softmax is over keys, not over queries

    The softmax in Equation 1 is applied row-wise: for each query, the weights across all keys sum to one. It is not a softmax over the query dimension, and it is not a softmax over the whole matrix. If you normalize the wrong axis, the rows will not sum to one and the output will not be a weighted average of the values.

    3.2.1 Scaled Dot-Product Attention. The attention used is called "Scaled Dot-Product Attention". The input consists of queries and keys of dimension d_k, and values of dimension d_v. The dot products of the query with all keys are computed, each is divided by sqrt(d_k), and a softmax function is applied to obtain the weights on the values. In practice the attention function is computed on a set of queries simultaneously, packed together into a matrix Q; the keys and values are packed into matrices K and V. The matrix of outputs is computed as: Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax(Q K^T / sqrt(d_k)) V (Equation 1). The two most commonly used attention functions are additive attention and dot-product (multiplicative) attention. Dot-product attention is identical to this algorithm except for the scaling factor 1/sqrt(d_k). Additive attention computes the compatibility function using a feed-forward network with a single hidden layer. The two are similar in theoretical complexity, but dot-product attention is faster and more space-efficient in practice because it can use optimized matrix multiplication. For small d_k the two mechanisms perform similarly, but additive attention outperforms dot-product attention without scaling for larger d_k. The authors suspect that for large d_k the dot products grow large in magnitude, pushing the softmax into regions where it has extremely small gradients; to counteract this, the dot products are scaled by 1/sqrt(d_k). Footnote 4: to illustrate why the dot products get large, assume the components of q and k are independent random variables with mean 0 and variance 1; then their dot product q . k = sum_{i=1}^{d_k} q_i k_i has mean 0 and variance d_k.
